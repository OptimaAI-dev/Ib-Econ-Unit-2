The top of your Unit 2 tab

Foundations

Two ideas that every Unit 2 model rests on: markets are assumed to be competitive, and consumers are assumed to be rational. Section 2.4 then asks what happens when that second assumption breaks.

Competition
The effect of a large number of buyers and sellers acting independently, so an individual seller has very little or no market power to influence the price of the product.
Market power
The ability of a firm (or group of firms) to raise and maintain prices above the level that would occur under competition.
Ceteris paribus added
“All other things being equal”: every other factor is held constant while one variable changes. It sits at the end of the definitions of demand, supply and both laws.
Assumptions about human nature and behaviour
  • Human beings have clear preferences that are stable over time, ceteris paribus
  • They have highly developed analytical skills and perfect knowledge, so they make rational choices
  • Rational behaviour means maximising personal satisfaction (utility) at all times
Why it matters on the test
  • These are the assumptions behind rational consumer choice, which is what makes the demand curve predictable
  • The matching producer assumption is profit maximisation added
  • Behavioural economics (2.4) is the critique: real people have bounded rationality, incomplete information and biases

Section 2.1 · Consumers

Demand

What consumers are willing and able to buy, why the curve slopes down, and what moves the whole curve.

What the test can askDefine demand and the law of demand. Sketch a movement along the curve against a shift of it. HL: explain the downward slope using diminishing marginal utility and the income and substitution effects. Know every demand shifter for the 10-point question.

2.1

Demand and the law of demand

What does the curve show?
Demand
The quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to purchase at various prices during a given period, ceteris paribus.
Law of demand
The principle that as the price of a product increases, the quantity demanded decreases (and as price falls, quantity demanded rises), ceteris paribus.
Effective demand
The quantity of goods or services that consumers are actually buying: a want backed by the ability to pay.
Change in quantity demanded
A movement along the demand curve, caused only by a change in the product's own price.
Change in demand
A shift of the entire curve, caused by a change in a non-price determinant. Right = increase, left = decrease.
The classic slip. “The price rose, so demand fell” loses the mark. Price changes quantity demanded (a movement). Demand only changes when a non-price determinant changes (a shift). Say “quantity demanded” whenever price is the cause.
2.1 HL

Why the demand curve slopes downward

HL explain
Income effect
When the price of a good that is being consumed falls, consumers experience an increase in real income, usually allowing them to buy more of it. The income effect may be negative (inferior goods).
Substitution effect
When the price of a product falls relative to other products, there is an incentive to buy more of it, because its marginal utility/price ratio has improved.
Marginal utility added
The extra satisfaction gained from consuming one more unit of a good.
Law of diminishing marginal utility blank in notes
As a person consumes more units of a good, the extra satisfaction (marginal utility) from each additional unit eventually falls, ceteris paribus.

The chain, in the order the marks come

Each extra slice of pizza gives less extra satisfaction than the one before: marginal utility diminishes.
A rational consumer only buys another unit if its marginal utility is worth the price, so they are willing to pay less for each additional unit.
So consumers will only buy a larger quantity at a lower price.
That inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded is the downward-sloping demand curve. The income and substitution effects push the same way when price falls.

Exam tip

Explain why the demand curve slopes downward. [4]

Model answer. The law of demand states that as price rises, quantity demanded falls, ceteris paribus [1]. One reason is diminishing marginal utility: each additional unit gives less extra satisfaction, so consumers are only willing to buy more units at a lower price [1]. Second, the substitution effect: when the price falls, the good becomes cheaper relative to substitutes, so its marginal utility per dollar improves and consumers switch towards it [1]. Third, the income effect: a lower price raises real income, so consumers can afford more of the good [1].

2.2

Supply and the law of supply

What does the curve show?
Producers
People, companies or countries that make, grow or supply goods, services or resources in a market. Producers come in many forms.
Supply
The quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to offer at various prices during a specific time period, ceteris paribus.
Law of supply
As the price of a product increases, the quantity supplied will usually increase, ceteris paribus.
Movement along the supply curve
A change in quantity supplied, caused only by a change in the product's own price.
Shift in supply
A change to a non-price determinant shifts supply left (decrease) or right (increase). It changes the quantity producers are willing and able to supply at every price.
Fix this line in your notes. Near the end of the Supply section it says “If price increases supply decreases.” That contradicts the law of supply. A price increase raises quantity supplied, which is a movement up the curve. Supply only decreases (shifts left) when a non-price determinant changes, for example when costs rise.

2.2 HL

Diminishing marginal returns and the supply curve

HL explain
Marginal returns
The additional returns (output/product) gained from adding one more unit of a factor of production (input).
Law of diminishing marginal returns
Adding more of one factor of production (input) while holding at least one other factor constant will, at some point, yield lower marginal returns (output/product). It is a short-run idea, because in the short run at least one factor is fixed.
Fixed capital
A real, physical asset that is used in the production of a product but is not used up in production: the pizza oven, not the dough.
Marginal cost
The cost of producing one more unit of a good.

Exam tip

Explain the relationship between the law of diminishing marginal returns and the shape of the short-run supply curve. [4]

Model answer.

  1. In the short run, at least one factor of production is fixed (the oven), so firms raise output by adding a variable factor (workers) [1].
  2. By the law of diminishing marginal returns, each extra worker eventually adds less extra output than the one before [1].
  3. So each additional unit of output needs more of the variable input, and marginal cost rises as output increases [1].
  4. A producer will only increase quantity supplied if it receives a higher price to cover the higher marginal cost, so the short-run supply curve slopes upward [1].

That last sentence is almost word-for-word your notes: “A producer will therefore only want to increase the quantity supplied if she can receive a higher price in the market.”

2.2

Capacity utilisation and optimisation

Using what you have
Capacity utilisation
How much of your total potential capacity you are actually using, usually as a percentage.
Capacity optimisation
The strategy to improve that percentage without overloading the system.
Together
Getting the most value out of business assets (machinery, staff, vehicles, software) by matching them to demand.

Link it to diminishing returns: the pizzeria at 7 workers has very high utilisation of its oven but terrible marginal returns. Optimisation means stopping around the point where the extra output still justifies the extra cost, or buying a second oven in the long run.

Competitive market equilibrium

What the test can askDefine equilibrium price and disequilibrium. Sketch a surplus and a shortage. Explain the signalling and incentive functions of price. The 10-point question lives here: a shifter from 2.1 or 2.2 followed all the way to a new equilibrium.

2.3

Equilibrium and disequilibrium

Where does the market settle?
Market
All the consumers willing and able to purchase a good at different prices and all the producers willing and able to supply it. In other words, a market is its demand and supply.
Market equilibrium
The point where the supply curve crosses the demand curve, at the price where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
Equilibrium price
The price set by the interaction between consumers and producers, where there are no surpluses or shortages and the market has cleared. Also called the market-clearing price.
Disequilibrium
Where quantity demanded does not equal quantity supplied. Markets spend most of their time here, but it is assumed to be temporary if prices can adjust.
Surplus
Excess supply: at any price above equilibrium, Qs > Qd. Prices fall to reach equilibrium.
Shortage
Excess demand: at any price below equilibrium, Qd > Qs. Prices rise to reach equilibrium.
2.3

The price mechanism and negative feedback loops

How does a market fix itself?
Price mechanism
The way price changes affect quantity demanded and quantity supplied, thus determining resource allocation in a market.
Negative feedback loop
The process that brings a market back to equilibrium after a shift in demand or supply. A price change triggers it in two ways: signals and incentives.
Signalling function
A change in price is a signal to consumers and producers to change their behaviour.
Incentive function
Consumers and producers are motivated to react in a way that brings the market back to equilibrium. For firms, the motive is maximising profit.

Your pizza example, cleaned up

Demand for pizza increases (D1 → D2). At the old price P1, quantity demanded is now bigger than quantity supplied: a shortage (excess demand).
Firms see the shortage and raise the price. The rising price is a signal; the incentive is profit, so quantity supplied extends up S while quantity demanded contracts along D2.
The market reaches a new equilibrium where Qd = Qs, at P2 and Q2. Both are higher than before.

The question

Explain how a change in a non-price determinant of demand and a change in a non-price determinant of supply could affect the equilibrium price and quantity in a market. [10]

The structure that scores

  1. Define demand, supply and equilibrium price, and say that non-price determinants shift the curves while the good's own price only moves along them.
  2. Diagram 1: the market at its starting equilibrium, P1 and Q1.
  3. Demand paragraph. Name the determinant and its direction → D shifts → shortage or surplus at P1 → price changes as a signal and incentive → movements along the curves → new P2 and Q2, referring to your diagram.
  4. Supply paragraph. The same chain with a supply determinant, on its own diagram.
  5. Close by naming the price mechanism: the price change reallocated resources until Qd = Qs again.

Worked example, the demand paragraph. Pizza is a normal good, so if consumers' incomes rise, demand increases and shifts right from D1 to D2 (Diagram 2). At the original price P1, quantity demanded now exceeds quantity supplied, creating a shortage. The shortage signals to producers that consumers value pizza more, and the higher price gives firms an incentive to supply more because it is now more profitable. Quantity supplied extends along S1 while quantity demanded contracts along D2, until a new equilibrium is reached at a higher price P2 and a higher quantity Q2.

Worked example, the supply paragraph. If the wages of pizza makers rise, the cost of production increases, so supply decreases and shifts left from S1 to S2 (Diagram 3). At P1 there is now a shortage, so the price rises. The higher price signals scarcity and rations the pizza among consumers, so quantity demanded contracts along D1. The new equilibrium is at a higher price P2 but a lower quantity Q2.

    Econs and Humans

    Who is the demand curve about?
    Behavioural economics

    System 1, System 2 and cognitive biases

    Why do Humans get it wrong?
    System 1
    • Thinking that comes automatically, with little effort and little or no control

    Exam tip

    Explain how cognitive biases may limit consumers' ability to make rational choices. [4]

    Model answer. A cognitive bias is a systematic error in thinking that makes decisions deviate from rationality [1]. Rational consumer choice assumes people weigh all the information to maximise utility, but Humans have bounded rationality: limited information, time and ability to calculate, so they rely on fast System 1 thinking [1]. For example, with anchoring, a consumer judges a $50 jacket as a bargain because the tag shows an original price of $80, even if $50 is more than it is worth to them [1]. So consumers can buy goods that do not maximise their utility, which means real demand may not match the predictions of the rational model [1].

    2.4

    Choice architecture and nudges

    Using biases on purpose
    Choice architecture
    The way choices are structured for consumers: the physical positioning of products, the language used to present a choice, the sequencing of choices and the range of offerings. Big picture: it uses biases to achieve a desired outcome.
    Nudge theory
    Any arrangement of choice architecture that alters people's choices without limiting choices or significantly changing incentives.
    The three types, with your class examples
    TypeDefinitionExamples from classThe bias it works with
    Default choiceAn option is automatically set for consumers, and stays unless they change it.New cars that turn off while idling at stop lights · printer settings · cookie settings · auto-renewed subscriptionsStatus quo / inertia: most people never change the default

    Exam tip

    Explain how choice architecture may take advantage of consumers' biases and bounded rationality. [4]

    Model answer. Choice architecture is the way choices are structured and presented to consumers, and it works because consumers are not fully rational [1]. With a default choice, such as a subscription that renews automatically, firms rely on inertia: many consumers never cancel, so they keep paying for a service they no longer use [1]. With restricted choice, such as a short restaurant menu, a business steers people towards the options it wants to sell, helping consumers whose bounded rationality makes a long list hard to compare [1]. Because these structures change behaviour without banning any option or changing prices much, they are nudges, and they can be used to help consumers (organ donation) or to profit from them (auto-renewals) [1].

    2.4

    Alternative business objectives

    What else might a firm want?
    Profit maximisation added
    The rational producer assumption: firms aim for the largest possible profit. Everything below is an alternative to it.
    Corporate social responsibility (CSR)
    A firm acting ethically and taking responsibility for its impact on society and the environment, even when that means not maximising profit. Your notes: “alternative business objectives are CSR”.
    Market share
    The percentage of a market that a firm controls.
    Growth
    Aiming to get bigger: more sales, more market share. It links to economies of scale.
    Economies of scale
    A firm's ability to produce with lower average costs when it grows in size.
    Satisficing
    Making choices that are satisfying, but not maximising: aiming for a satisfactory level of profit rather than the maximum. (Your notes spell it “satisfying” once; the term is satisficing.)
    Fair trade
    Paying producers, usually in developing countries, a fair minimum price with decent working conditions; a firm may choose it as part of CSR.
    2.4

    Pricing strategies

    Low prices on purpose

    The link your notes make: all three strategies sacrifice profit now in order to maximise market share, which is why they belong with alternative business objectives rather than with profit maximisation.

    2.4

    Costs and benefits of CSR

    Short run against long run
    Short run: the costs
    • Higher costs: ethical suppliers, cleaner inputs, fair wages
    • Higher research and development costs
    • Less profit: the firm is not maximising profit
    Long run: the benefits
    • Reduces costs borne by society and by future businesses (less pollution to clean up, healthier workers)
    • Growth and market share: ethical consumers switch to the firm
    • A stronger brand and reputation added

    Exam tip

    Discuss the costs and benefits to a firm of pursuing corporate social responsibility.

    How to make it a discussion, not a list. Define CSR, then set the short-run costs (higher costs, R&D, lower profit) against the long-run benefits (lower costs to society, growth, market share). The judgement is about time: CSR looks like a loss in the short run and can pay off in the long run, but only if consumers notice and value it. A firm in a very price-competitive market may not survive the short run long enough to see the benefit.

    Demand is what consumers are willing and able to buy at various prices over a period. Effective demand is what they are actually buying: a want backed by the ability to pay.

    The price of pizza falls. Does the demand curve for pizza shift?[1]

    No. It is a movement down the curve: quantity demanded rises. Only non-price determinants shift demand.

    Name five non-price determinants of demand.[2]

    Tastes and preferences · income (normal and inferior goods) · future expectations · prices of related goods (substitutes and complements) · demographics / number of consumers.

    Income rises and demand for a good falls. What kind of good is it?[1]

    An inferior good: consumers switch to better alternatives as they get richer.

    State the law of diminishing marginal returns.[2]

    Adding more of one factor of production while holding at least one other factor constant [1] will at some point yield lower marginal returns (extra output) [1].

    Marginal product falls from 20 to 15. Is total product falling?[1]

    No. Total product still rises by 15. It only falls if marginal product becomes negative.

    Why does diminishing marginal returns make the short-run supply curve slope upward?[2]

    Each extra unit of output needs more of the variable factor, so marginal cost rises [1]; firms will only supply more if the price rises enough to cover it [1].

    The price is above equilibrium. Surplus or shortage, and what happens next?[2]

    A surplus (excess supply) [1]. Sellers cut prices to clear stock, quantity demanded extends and quantity supplied contracts until Qd = Qs [1].

    Which two functions of price drive a negative feedback loop?[2]

    The signalling function (the price change tells buyers and sellers to change behaviour) and the incentive function (they are motivated to respond, for firms by profit).

    Supply decreases. What happens to equilibrium price and quantity?[2]

    Price rises [1], quantity falls [1]. At the old price there is a shortage, which pushes the price up.

    Demand and supply both increase. Which result is certain?[1]

    Quantity rises. The price could rise, fall or stay the same depending on which shift is bigger.

    Define anchoring bias and give the class example.[2]

    Relying too heavily on the first piece of information given (the anchor) [1]. Asking whether a student scored more or less than 850 on the SAT produces lower guesses than asking about 1420 [1].

    Which bias do default choices rely on?[2]

    Status quo / inertia bias [1]: most people stick with whatever is already set, so the default becomes the outcome (auto-renewed subscriptions, cookie settings) [1].

    Why is “masks during the pandemic” not an example of mandated choice?[1]

    It was a rule with no decision to state. Mandated choice forces people to make a choice, like saying yes or no to organ donation.

    Define satisficing.[1]

    Making choices that are satisfying but not maximising, such as aiming for a satisfactory profit rather than the maximum.

    Distinguish penetration pricing from predatory pricing.[2]

    Penetration pricing sets a low launch price to win customers and market share, then raises it [1]. Predatory pricing sets extremely low prices to drive competitors out, then raises prices using the market power gained [1].

    One short-run cost and one long-run benefit of CSR?[2]

    Short run: higher costs (including R&D) and lower profit [1]. Long run: lower costs borne by society and future businesses, or growth in market share [1].
